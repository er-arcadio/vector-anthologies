# Eli Reyes — Character Journey Worksheet

A filled instance of `../../writing-guide/character-journey-worksheet.md` for Eli, persisted here because he's the POV/major character for nearly everything in `stories/` and re-deriving this per story invites drift. Check this before outlining any Eli-POV story; update it if a real decision changes his want/need/arc, not casually mid-draft.

This is the series-level version. The per-phase breakdown below maps it onto `eli-character-arc.md`'s five phases so a story set in a specific arc can pull the right slice instead of the whole series arc.

---

## Series-level worksheet

- **Want:** To be enough — to master his power so completely that nothing can catch him short again: not his father's judgment, not his own moral failures, not the next threat. Control as the answer to every form of fear.
- **Need:** To stop mistaking control for safety or for connection. To become his own moral authority instead of performing for his father's (or the world's) approval, and to let himself be *present* in his own life rather than perpetually managing it.
- **The gap:** The more of the want he gets, the further he drifts from the need — mastering the world is not the same skill as inhabiting it, and for most of the story he can't tell the difference. Escalating power makes this worse, not better, until Arc 6 forces the reckoning.
- **Starting state (Arc 1, age 16):** Guarded, hides everything (feelings first, powers second — the second is just a bigger version of the first). Believes being loved/approved of requires flawless performance. Hard time having fun; insecure about losses, awkward about wins.
- **Ending state (Arc 7, age 27):** Participates rather than controls. Tells his father the truth without needing a particular response back. Can let something stay unresolved (Vinny's disappearance) without needing to file it away or solve it. Moral authority is his own, not borrowed.
- **Stakes if he fails the want (mastery):** People he could have protected get hurt or die — civilians, and eventually people he knows personally. Exposure before he's ready for it.
- **Cost if he succeeds at the want:** Emotional flatness. By Arc 6 he is close to the ceiling of what mastery can even mean and finds it hollow — "the hunger that drove him finds less to grab onto." Total control doesn't protect the people he loves most; his mother's death is the proof that it can't.
- **Cost if he gets the need instead:** He has to give up the illusion that competence equals safety. He has to let people see him uncertain, has to accept some things (grief, Vinny, the shape of his own guilt) don't resolve into a clean mechanism no matter how well he understands the world.

**Strengths → obstacle type they're naturally equipped for:**
- Strength 1: Rapid technical mastery, pattern recognition, a power ceiling that scales with genuine comprehension → handles **external** obstacles (villains, technical crises, containment problems) exceptionally well.

**Weaknesses → obstacle type the plot should specifically target:**
- Weakness 1: Cause-and-effect mind applied to problems that don't have a mechanism — as a teenager he is not yet emotionally literate and doesn't have the vocabulary or self-awareness to name what he feels, only what happened and what it caused → **internal** and **interpersonal** obstacles (grief, love, ambiguity, a guilt that can't be solved) are what should actually catch him.
- Weakness 2 (optional): Perfectionism and fear of failure, inherited directly from his father's voice → **internal** obstacle; this is also the seed of the playing-god temptation — if failure is unbearable, omnipotence starts to look like the only safe answer.

- **The weakness is load-bearing:** The Shockwave killing (Arc 5) is exactly this failure mode in action — under maximum pressure, his cause-and-effect training reaches for the tool he's drilled (destroy the threat) rather than the untested, ambiguous, humane one (attempt to restructure). The plot doesn't let him have both a clean conscience and a fast solution; the story's whole tragic architecture runs through this gap.
- **The strength has a shadow side:** His comprehension-based mastery over the physical world tempts him to treat human and moral problems as physics problems too — explicit existing canon in Arc 3, where he "treats human consequences as a technical problem" during the blackout. The same mind that makes him exceptional is what makes him occasionally, dangerously obtuse about people.

**Relationship check:**
- Friction: Iris refuses to let him perform for her the way everyone else lets him — this puts his central strategy (control the read people get of you) in direct conflict with what she wants from him (to just be Eli), and it's a friction that doesn't resolve until he stops needing to win it.
- Synergy tested: His mother is the one person who holds both versions of him without contradiction — the one relationship where the want and the need aren't in tension. That synergy is tested precisely by taking her away with no mechanism to blame, no villain, nothing his want (control) could ever have prevented. It's the story's proof that mastery was never going to be enough.

## Per-phase quick reference

Maps onto `eli-character-arc.md`. Use the relevant row when outlining a story set in that phase — it's the localized version of the want/need/obstacle above, not a new one.

| Phase | Arcs | Local want | Local obstacle | What "resolution" costs him |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Containment | Arc 1 (16) | Keep the evidence contained — powers are one more thing to perfect, one more way to fail if seen | Internal: shame/fear of exposure, not yet any external villain worth the name | Tells his mother — the relief of being known, but the secret itself narrows who he can be known by |
| 2. Justification | Arcs 2–3 (17.5–21) | Prove the power is used *rightly* — fighting evil as something his father could approve of without reservation | External (villains, contained one per arc) masking an internal one: he hasn't yet separated "right" from "what earns approval" | Wins the fights, misses the people — the blackout, the Jade/Sol pattern: technically correct, humanly clumsy |
| 3. Fracture | Arcs 4–5 (22) | Hold the compartments apart — hero life, romantic life, moral cost — so none of them touch | Internal: the killing forces a moral question his cause-and-effect mind has no clean answer for | Shockwave dies. The "correct decision" doesn't feel correct. First glimpse that comprehension has a ceiling conscience can't get past in time |
| 4. Dissolution | Arc 6 (23–26) | Find something worth wanting, now that mastery hasn't delivered what he expected | Internal (apathy, "what is all of this for") colliding with external loss (mother's death — a problem no power touches) | Everything arrives at once; he runs to Colombia, and staying there (not the running) is the first real choice toward the need instead of the want |
| 5. Integration | Arc 7 (27) | Participate instead of control — fight because it's right, not because anyone's watching, including his father | Vinny — the hardest external opponent — but the real test is internal: can he let something end unresolved without reaching to fix it | Tells his father everything, no guarantee of the response. Stands in Vinny's unfinished ending without filing it away |
