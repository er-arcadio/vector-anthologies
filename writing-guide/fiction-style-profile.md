# Fiction Style Profile

**Role in this repo:** this is the reader-preference research behind the prose defaults in `style-guide.md` — specifically the sustained-dialogue default and the "quiet imagery over showy imagery" rule. Where the two ever disagree, `style-guide.md` is the one to follow (it's Vector-specific and deliberate); this file is the reasoning trail that produced it, kept intact rather than split up so the comparisons between books stay legible.

*A working document tracking what I actually respond to in prose, built by ranking real books against each other rather than describing taste in the abstract.*

## The Core Rule

**I favor prose that commits fully to one mode at a time — sustained dialogue or sustained narration — without breaking its own throughline to switch modes or stop and showcase a metaphor.**

This turned out to be more accurate than "I dislike metaphor" or "I dislike interruption" on their own. The real rule has two parts:

1. **Continuity over switching.** A scene that alternates rapidly between a line of dialogue and a beat of narration, over and over, loses me — even when each individual piece is short and simple. A scene that stays in one mode for a sustained stretch, dialogue or narration, keeps me in it.
2. **Narrative weight, not presence, of figurative language.** I don't actually mind metaphor or lyricism. What loses me is language that stops the sentence and asks to be admired on its own. What I glide past easily is imagery folded quietly into a working sentence, where it's supporting the scene rather than separate from it.

A secondary, minor finding: **unfamiliar dialect in dialogue is a tolerable cost, not a dealbreaker**, when the underlying structural rhythm is one I like. It adds a layer of decoding, but it doesn't undo the enjoyment the way mode-switching or showy metaphor does.

## Confirmed Likes

### George V. Higgins — *The Friends of Eddie Coyle*
The clearest hit so far. Long, unbroken stretches of dialogue with flat, functional narration in between — some scenes run almost entirely on dialogue with barely any narrative interruption. This is the book that made the "continuity over switching" rule click. The regional dialect added friction, but I could feel myself wanting to keep reading anyway, which separated "hard to parse" from "hard to enjoy."

### Megan Abbott — *Dare Me*
Ranked #1 in an early round. Liked the mix of introspection and dialogue, and the small-mystery-then-reveal rhythm. Flagged real content bias here — genuinely wanted to finish the book, which is a stronger signal than style alone.

### Kate Atkinson — *Case Histories*
Close second early on. Straightforward, easy to move through, no metaphor to stop and interpret along the way. This was an early, cruder version of what later got refined into the "narrative weight" rule — at the time it read as "no metaphors," but *The Secret History* later showed the real issue is showy metaphor, not metaphor itself.

### Curtis Sittenfeld — *Prep*
Ranked #1 in the "closed institution" round. Content (relatable high-school embarrassment) is a likely confound, but the structural point held up independently: short dialogue bursts read easily here specifically *because* they weren't interrupted mid-exchange by narration, unlike the Attica Locke sample below.

### Donna Tartt — *The Secret History*
Ranked #2 in the same round, with no dialogue in the sample at all. Strong hook, easy to move through despite real lyricism and some obscure metaphors — I found myself skimming past the more obscure ones without losing the thread, because the surrounding prose carried its own weight and didn't lean on the metaphor to do the work. This result is what overturned the earlier, simpler "avoid metaphor" theory.

### Wilkie Collins — *The Woman in White*
Direct, methodical, almost testimonial in voice. Comedic prose showed up here as a genuine plus — unexpected, since comedy hadn't been part of the profile yet. Flagged that the "pilgrims in London" subject matter may have suppressed the ranking; the prose itself was enjoyed more than the topic.

## Confirmed Dislikes

### Tana French — *In the Woods*
Run-on sentences, heavy analogy, hard to track what's actually happening underneath the language. First real signal against dense figurative language — later refined (see *The Secret History*, above) to specifically mean figurative language that carries too much narrative weight on its own, rather than metaphor as a category.

### Denis Johnson — *Jesus' Son*
Short sentences, but still hard to read. Useful negative result: it proved the issue with French wasn't just "long winding sentences," since Johnson is short and punchy and still didn't work. Confirmed the aversion is about disorientation and imagery pulling focus, not sentence length.

### Attica Locke — *Bluebird, Bluebird*
Verified against the actual opening chapter: dialogue arrives one line at a time, each line separated by a beat of narration, repeating through the scene. This is the clearest real-world example of "mode-switching" as a concrete, checkable pattern rather than a vague impression — the excerpt matched the complaint exactly.

### Liane Moriarty — *Big Little Lies*
Too much back-and-forth between dialogue and narration, no time to settle into either one before the switch. This is what first surfaced the "dwell time" idea, later folded into the core rule.

### Richard Price — *Clockers*
Predicted to struggle for the same reason as *Bluebird, Bluebird* — narration weaves in and out within dialogue exchanges rather than staying out of the way. Confirmed: disliked, alongside *Out of Sight* in the same round (see note below).

## Mixed / Inconclusive

- **Elmore Leonard — *Out of Sight*:** Preferred over *Clockers*, which fits the theory (long, largely uninterrupted dialogue scenes). But the comparison is compromised — I'd already seen the film adaptation, so the preference may reflect familiarity with the story rather than the prose itself. Treat as a weak positive, not a confirmed hit.
- **Karin Slaughter — *Pretty Girls*:** "Good not great." Simple dialogue, but scene-painting and action sequences felt sporadic. Ranked 3rd of 5 in that round — plausible mid-tier fit rather than a strong signal either way.
- **Gillian Flynn — *Sharp Objects*** and **S.A. Cosby — *Razorblade Tears*:** Tied, both "fine to read," both content-neutral. Useful as a control (isolating prose from content interest) but not strong enough on their own to confirm or rule out anything new.
- **Carl Hiaasen — *Skinny Dip*:** "Not that funny," but readable, and does spend sustained time in narration before shifting into dialogue — consistent with the core rule even though the comedic angle underwhelmed.

## Genre and Setting Notes

- **Genre itself doesn't appear to drive the preference** — style traits (directness, mode-continuity, quiet vs. showy imagery) have shown up across mystery, literary fiction, and crime without a clear genre pattern.
- **Period-piece European settings are a separate, real aversion** — distinct from prose style. Likely tied to unfamiliarity or lack of relatability with "old-world, formal" settings (Collins' London, Brontë's moors) rather than Europe as a place. Contemporary European fiction is untested and may not carry the same aversion — worth checking directly rather than assuming.
- **Content curiosity is a persistent, correctly self-flagged confound** throughout this entire process (Abbott, Collins, Waters, Prep). Style and content interest keep entangling, and the ranking method hasn't fully separated them — worth remembering when reading new recommendations that a "miss" might be about the story, not the sentences.

## Open Questions / Next Tests

- Is the "unfamiliar dialect" tolerance specific to Higgins' style of dialect, or does it hold for other regional/vernacular voices?
- Does the core rule (continuity + quiet imagery) hold in a genre outside mystery/crime/literary fiction — sci-fi, memoir, contemporary romance?
- Is "contemporary European" actually fine, or does some version of the period-piece aversion persist regardless of publication date?
- *Out of Sight* needs a clean replacement test, unbiased by the film, to confirm Leonard's style specifically (rather than Higgins-adjacent authors generally) is a real hit.
