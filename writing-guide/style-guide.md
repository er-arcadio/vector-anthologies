# Style Guide

Consolidated from the original story bible's tone/genre notes and the craft instructions embedded throughout the arc-by-arc outline. Apply this to any short story added to `stories/`.

## Format

Prose first, always. Novel/short-story form takes priority over any illustrated or hybrid treatment. A double-page illustration per chapter is a possible later layer — never a substitute for the prose doing the work.

## Length — two kinds of piece

Every story in `stories/` declares which kind it is in its frontmatter (`story_type`, see `../stories/CLAUDE.md`). Move between the two freely, piece by piece — but **arc-advancing stories are the default and should outnumber single-beat pieces.**

**Arc-advancing story — target 3,000–5,000 words.** The main form. Carries a protagonist from a real point A to a real point B with a want and an obstacle, and moves the arc it sits in. The range is wide on purpose: long enough for genuine scene-level development, short enough to draft, finish, and evaluate in a sitting or two. Room in that range goes toward earning the emotional turn — a scene that needs to breathe, a beat that needs setup — never toward padding to clear the floor. Running past 5,000 isn't wrong; flag it and decide deliberately whether it's a short story stretching or the start of something arc-length.

**Single-beat piece — no floor, typically 800–1,500 words.** A deliberately small form for beats whose whole power is compression, where added length would actively destroy them. Several beats in `story-bible/plot-outline/era-1-overview.md` are designed this way on purpose — Theo's death handled in two sentences with no mourning scene, the diner scene where nothing happens, the arc where Vinny simply doesn't appear. A single-beat piece still needs a reason to exist and an effect it's going for; what it doesn't need is an A-to-B journey or a word count. Choosing this form is a real choice, not a shortfall, and doesn't require a defense — but choosing it *because the draft ran short* does. If a piece was conceived as arc-advancing and landed at 1,600 words, that's a flag, not a reclassification.

Both are separate from the eventual novel-length prose the `story-bible/` arcs are outlined for.

## Every story needs an A and a B

*(Applies to arc-advancing stories. A single-beat piece is exempt by definition — see Length above.)*

An arc-advancing story is not a mood or an image — it needs a protagonist moving from a starting point (A) to a changed point (B), a want, and an obstacle (external, interpersonal, or internal) actually standing between them. **Point A and Point B are concrete states** — where/who the protagonist is at the start and at the end, a real before-and-after — not a pair of abstract lessons or themes the story illustrates. If A and B can't each be stated as a situation or condition ("guarded, hiding the power" → "told his mother, first taste of trusting someone with it"), they aren't a real A and B yet. This is what `outline-checklist.md` and `character-journey-worksheet.md` exist to nail down before drafting — run them for real, don't skip the step because the piece feels small. Record the result with the story (see the "Story Shape" block required by `../stories/CLAUDE.md`) so the want/need/obstacle/resolution are visible and reviewable, not just something the outline pass privately assumed.

## Tone blend (target ratio)

- **60% Action Drama** — grounded coming-of-age material: responsibility, grief, identity, releasing inherited expectations, real love, finding purpose.
- **30% Visceral Action** — violence that shocks because it's earned, not decorative. Every major fight should leave a permanent mark on someone or something.
- **10% Horror** — present as a background hum, not a genre the story lives in. A constant low presence that occasionally spikes to full volume, then recedes. Light comedy in human moments provides contrast against it.

Adult content, including sex scenes, is permitted where it serves character arc — never as decoration.

## Narrative voice

- **Retrospective first person, rendered in present tense by default.** An older, integrated version of the POV character is telling this story after the fact — that's who is narrating, and the gap between who's narrating and who's being depicted is still part of the character arc. But the prose itself defaults to present tense (a "historical present"), so the reader moves through events as they happen rather than sitting at the narrator's remove the whole time. Retrospective is the narrator's identity; present tense is the default register they tell it in.
- **Past tense is circumstantial, not a scheduled exception.** Use it when the narrator explicitly steps back into reflection — on something already resolved, or recounting/telling a story within the story (relaying an earlier event to someone else, a remembered scene framed as memory). When past tense is doing this work, let the gap between the narrating self and the remembered self show on the page rather than explaining it outright — this is the retrospective distance becoming visible. Don't reserve tense shifts for "big" moments on a schedule — the choice should track a real in-scene reason to look back, not a checklist.
- **Present-tense breaks, still real.** Don't let the story's most overwhelming beats get told at reflective distance. If a passage has drifted into past-tense reflection, its most alive moment should snap back into present tense — the narrator briefly losing control of his own distance. Present tense is already the default everywhere else, so in practice this rule mostly matters *inside* a past-tense passage: it's where a flashback or told-story stops being safely distant and becomes immediate again.
- **Eli's age-appropriate interiority, distinct from the narrator's polish.** The retrospective narrator is an older, integrated Eli — his telling voice can be more articulate than any version of him ever actually was. But in scenes rendered close to a *young* Eli (Arcs 1–4, ages 16–21), his in-scene thoughts, self-understanding, and vocabulary should read like an actual teenager's, not the narrator's hindsight leaking down into the character being depicted. See `story-bible/characters/eli-reyes.md` for the full characterization this is drawn from (STEM-brilliant, cause-and-effect minded, not yet emotionally literate) — this is the resulting prose rule.
  - **Fixation over even attention.** A teenager's focus goes disproportionately to what he already cares about, not evenly to whatever the plot considers important. Let young Eli's narration linger on those fixations and skate past things he doesn't yet register as significant — even when the reader can tell they are. Don't let him notice his own undercurrents faster than a real teenager would.
  - **What he fixates on, ranked.** He has a full emotional range, not just the two below — but of everything competing for his attention, **fun** (friends, sex, a fight he's replaying because it was satisfying, a piece of gear he's proud of — anything actually enjoyable) gets disproportionate page-time and interiority. Dread and anxiety (below) get comparatively *less* — present, real, but not what he's dwelling on by default. A real teenager under-indexes on the things he's obligated to feel bad about and over-indexes on what he likes; his narration should too.
  - **Dread vs. anxiety — not interchangeable, and both lower-emphasis than fun.** When either does surface, which one is diagnostic of what he actually understands. **Anxiety** is for anything he already recognizes as carrying moral or responsibility weight — tight, specific, guilt-adjacent (a person he could have saved, a choice he knows is his). **Dread** is for an obligation whose weight he hasn't yet connected to responsibility — a vague, low unease about something he's avoiding without fully knowing why. As he matures across the arcs, things that used to register as dread should start registering as anxiety, and both should start taking up more real page-time relative to fun — that shift in emphasis is a way to show growth without narrating it directly.
- **Omniscient narrator — a wise, warm, extremely well-read woman**, not a detached camera. Picture someone who has read everything, lived a very long time, and forgotten none of it: equal parts English professor and historian, with real technical vocabulary and real affection for everyone she's ever taught. She is objective in that she never tells the reader who to root for and extends the same patient curiosity to a villain mid-atrocity as to a hero mid-sacrifice — but she is not neutral on cruelty, only nonjudgmental about its causes; she can find a monster's logic coherent and still let his victim's fear land in full.
  - **Vocabulary spent charmingly — she's telling this to her grandchildren.** That's the exact register: she reaches for the precise word — anatomical, historical, psychological — because precision matters to her, and then she breaks it down because the kids listening simply haven't met that word yet. She is *excited* to help. There is no contempt anywhere in the engine: not "you wouldn't understand this," but "oh, and here's what that one means — isn't that a good word for it." When a term or idea is genuinely obscure she glosses it in the same breath, briefly and warmly, folded into the sentence. Never a footnote, never a talking-down.
  - **Patient pace.** She takes her time: breaking a hard idea into its parts before moving on, letting a heavy moment breathe rather than rushing to the next beat. This is a deliberate contrast with the compressed, cause-and-effect economy of Eli's own present-tense sections — the omniscient voice is where the story allows itself room.
  - **Unflinching, not shocked.** She is telling a genuinely dark, at points explicit story — violence, death, sex, the worst things people do to each other and to themselves — without flinching from it and without performing shock at it either. Her composure isn't coldness; it's the calm of someone who has seen a very great deal and still cares about every person she's telling you about.
  - **Flat delivery for horror, specifically.** For antagonist interiority or scenes Eli isn't present for, and above all for the horror-hum beats: flat, even prose. Wrong or unsettling details are treated as ordinary — no dramatic emphasis, no dread music. The horror lives in the flatness, not in the framing — this is that same warm, patient narrator choosing not to editorialize, not a colder voice taking over.

## The horror register — how to write the hum

This is the hardest instruction to get right and the easiest to overdo. Rules of thumb pulled directly from how it was handled across the Vector arcs:

- **Plant without announcing.** A wrong detail (a shadow that moves slightly off, a clock that's always a little wrong) gets one flat sentence and no follow-up. Do not explain it in the same scene. Let the reader notice before the narration does.
- **Absence over performance.** The most effective horror/grief beats in this story are the ones with no mourning scene, no interior monologue, no transition marking a loss (a death handled in two sentences with no pause in the prose is more devastating than three paragraphs of reaction). Resist the instinct to dramatize; let plainness carry the weight.
- **Never confirm ambiguous threads unless the plot requires it.** Some questions are more powerful unresolved. Don't tie a bow on something that was designed to sit with the reader.
- **Comedy is a release valve, not a tone shift.** Light human-moment comedy should exist to make the hum land harder by contrast, not to lighten the story's actual direction.

## Dialogue

- **Sparse and sustained — not either/or.** Keep individual lines terse and unexplained: subtext should live in word choice and deflection, not in narration stepping in to interpret. And once a scene is in dialogue, stay in it — let the exchange run for a real stretch rather than cutting back to narration between nearly every line. These are different axes (how much any one line says vs. how often narration interrupts), and the model for having both at once is Higgins-style dialogue: short, clipped lines, run consecutively with almost no narration between them. See `fiction-style-profile.md` for the reasoning behind the sustained-exchange half of this.
- The most important conversations are often the ones that "never say what they're actually about." Don't over-explain a relationship in dialogue that's already been established through action.
- Reserve a narration beat for when it's actually doing work (a physical action, a pause that changes the scene) — not as a rhythmic habit between lines.

## Fight writing

- Every ability used should visibly affect its environment — nothing is clean or silent (see `story-bible/power-system/abilities-environmental-effects.md` for the specific physical consequences of each power).
- A fight's stakes should be traceable to character, not just spectacle — what does this specific confrontation cost, reveal, or force a character to choose that a different opponent wouldn't?
- Restraint is more dramatically interesting than escalation. A character choosing not to use their strongest option, and what that choice costs them, is worth more page time than the option itself.

## Research anchor

Wherever a power or its consequence is described, it should trace to something real (physics, anatomy, neuroscience) — flavor text is a last resort, not a first draft. See `story-bible/power-system/physical-consequences-and-canon.md` for the standard this was held to.
