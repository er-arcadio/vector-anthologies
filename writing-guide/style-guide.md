# Style Guide

Consolidated from the original story bible's tone/genre notes and the craft instructions embedded throughout the arc-by-arc outline. Apply this to any short story added to `stories/`.

## Format

Prose first, always. Novel/short-story form takes priority over any illustrated or hybrid treatment. A double-page illustration per chapter is a possible later layer — never a substitute for the prose doing the work.

## Tone blend (target ratio)

- **60% Action Drama** — grounded coming-of-age material: responsibility, grief, identity, releasing inherited expectations, real love, finding purpose.
- **30% Visceral Action** — violence that shocks because it's earned, not decorative. Every major fight should leave a permanent mark on someone or something.
- **10% Horror** — present as a background hum, not a genre the story lives in. A constant low presence that occasionally spikes to full volume, then recedes. Light comedy in human moments provides contrast against it.

Adult content, including sex scenes, is permitted where it serves character arc — never as decoration.

## Narrative voice

- **Present tense is the default** for POV-holding protagonists. The reader moves through events as the character experiences them, not from a distanced retrospective vantage.
- **Past tense is circumstantial, not a scheduled exception.** Use it when the narrator is explicitly reflecting back on something already resolved, or recounting/telling a story within the story (relaying an earlier event to someone else, a remembered scene framed as memory). When past tense is doing this work, let the gap between the narrating self and the remembered self show on the page rather than explaining it outright. Don't reserve tense shifts for "big" moments on a schedule — the choice should track a real in-scene reason to look back, not a checklist.
- **Omniscient narrator**, used for antagonist interiority or scenes the protagonist isn't present for: flat, even prose. Wrong or unsettling details are treated as ordinary — no dramatic emphasis, no dread music. The horror lives in the flatness, not in the framing.

## The horror register — how to write the hum

This is the hardest instruction to get right and the easiest to overdo. Rules of thumb pulled directly from how it was handled across the Vector arcs:

- **Plant without announcing.** A wrong detail (a shadow that moves slightly off, a clock that's always a little wrong) gets one flat sentence and no follow-up. Do not explain it in the same scene. Let the reader notice before the narration does.
- **Absence over performance.** The most effective horror/grief beats in this story are the ones with no mourning scene, no interior monologue, no transition marking a loss (a death handled in two sentences with no pause in the prose is more devastating than three paragraphs of reaction). Resist the instinct to dramatize; let plainness carry the weight.
- **Never confirm ambiguous threads unless the plot requires it.** Some questions are more powerful unresolved. Don't tie a bow on something that was designed to sit with the reader.
- **Comedy is a release valve, not a tone shift.** Light human-moment comedy should exist to make the hum land harder by contrast, not to lighten the story's actual direction.

## Dialogue

- **Default to sustained, uninterrupted exchanges.** Once a scene is in dialogue, stay in it — let a conversation run for a real stretch rather than cutting back to narration between nearly every line. Save a narration beat for when it's actually doing work (a physical action, a pause that changes the scene), not as a rhythmic habit between exchanges. See `fiction-style-profile.md` for the reasoning behind this default.
- **Subtext still rules** — the most important conversations are often the ones that "never say what they're actually about." But that subtext should live in the dialogue itself (word choice, deflection, what's left unsaid) rather than being supplied by narration commentary wedged between lines.
- Don't over-explain a relationship in dialogue that's already been established through action.

## Fight writing

- Every ability used should visibly affect its environment — nothing is clean or silent (see `story-bible/power-system/abilities-environmental-effects.md` for the specific physical consequences of each power).
- A fight's stakes should be traceable to character, not just spectacle — what does this specific confrontation cost, reveal, or force a character to choose that a different opponent wouldn't?
- Restraint is more dramatically interesting than escalation. A character choosing not to use their strongest option, and what that choice costs them, is worth more page time than the option itself.

## Research anchor

Wherever a power or its consequence is described, it should trace to something real (physics, anatomy, neuroscience) — flavor text is a last resort, not a first draft. See `story-bible/power-system/physical-consequences-and-canon.md` for the standard this was held to.
