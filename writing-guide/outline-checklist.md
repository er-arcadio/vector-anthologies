# Story Outline Checklist: Designing Event Sequences

A tool for the "this happened, then this happened" stage — before scenes are written, while you're still deciding *what occurs and in what order*. Each event on your outline should pass these checks before you move to the next one.

Fill out `character-journey-worksheet.md` per POV/major character first — it's referenced below as the check for individual beats.

---

## 1. Before You Sequence Anything

- [ ] **You know the ending.** Not the exact prose, but the final state: who's changed, what's true now that wasn't before, what was won and what was lost.
- [ ] **You know the want vs. the need.** Write both down separately (see `character-journey-worksheet.md`). The gap between them is your spine.
- [ ] **You know the central question.** One sentence, answerable only at the end (e.g., "Will she choose the throne or her sister?").
- [ ] **You've picked your twist(s), if any, and their reveal points.** Knowing the destination lets you plant clues on the way there instead of retrofitting them.

---

## 2. For Each Event in the Sequence

Ask of every beat: *what does this event change?* If the honest answer is "nothing," cut it or merge it into a neighbor.

- [ ] **Changes the situation** — new information, new obstacle, new alliance/betrayal, a door closed or opened.
- [ ] **Changes the character** — a belief tested, a flaw exposed, a choice made under pressure.
- [ ] **Advances OR complicates the goal** — not just "makes progress." A step backward or sideways that costs something is often more interesting than a clean win.
- [ ] **Is caused by the event before it, not just followed by it.** Test this by replacing "and then" with "therefore" or "but" in your outline. If "and then" is the only word that fits, the sequence is a list, not a story.
- [ ] **Could only happen because of who this character specifically is.** If you could swap the protagonist for anyone else and the event plays out the same way, the event isn't doing character work yet.

---

## 3. Causality Check (the "therefore/but" test)

Write your outline as a single paragraph using only **"therefore"** and **"but"** to connect events — never "and then." If you get stuck and *have* to write "and then," that joint is weak.

> She confronts the smuggler, **but** he denies everything convincingly. **Therefore** she breaks into his ledger room that night. **But** the ledgers are in a cipher she can't read. **Therefore** she has to ask the one person who taught her ciphers — her estranged brother.

This test alone catches most "flat" outlines: sequences where things simply occur one after another with no pressure pushing from one to the next.

---

## 4. Obstacle Variety Pass

Look at your full sequence and tag each obstacle:

- [ ] **External** (environment, enemies, time pressure, logistics)
- [ ] **Interpersonal** (another goal-holder in the way, or an ally with different priorities)
- [ ] **Internal** (the character's own flaw, fear, or blind spot is the actual obstacle)

- [ ] No more than 2–3 consecutive beats share the *same* obstacle type. Variety keeps the sequence from feeling grindy even in outline form.
- [ ] At least one major obstacle is caused, in part, by the protagonist's own weakness — not just bad luck or an external villain.

---

## 5. Escalation Check

- [ ] Stakes rise across the sequence — not necessarily "bigger explosions," but *higher cost of failure* or *narrower options*.
- [ ] At least one event **reframes** an earlier event (a reversal) rather than simply adding a new problem on top.
- [ ] The final third of the sequence removes an option or safety net the character has been relying on. They can't solve the climax the way they solved earlier problems — that's what forces growth.

---

## 6. Choice Points

- [ ] At least 2–3 events in the sequence are **decisions**, not just challenges. Mark them explicitly in the outline (e.g., `[CHOICE]`).
- [ ] Each choice has two options that are both genuinely costly — if one option is obviously correct, it isn't a choice, it's a test of comprehension.
- [ ] At least one choice forces want vs. need into direct conflict.

---

## 7. NPC / Secondary Character Function

For every non-protagonist who appears in the sequence:

- [ ] **Their goal is stated**, even briefly, even if it never makes it into prose — you should know it.
- [ ] They function as **gate, guide, mirror, or contrast** — not just scenery. ("Mirror" = shares the protagonist's flaw; "contrast" = represents the road not taken.)
- [ ] Their goal creates friction with the protagonist's goal at least once, even if they're allies overall. Total agreement between characters is rarely interesting for long.

---

## 8. Twist Planting (if applicable)

- [ ] The twist **recontextualizes** something already shown — a goal, a relationship, or the protagonist themselves — rather than introducing new information cold.
- [ ] You've identified the "natural wrong assumption" the sequence invites the reader to make, and confirmed you never state anything **false** to support it — only true statements with a misleading implication.
- [ ] You've placed 2–3 clue touchpoints across the sequence, increasing in visibility, with the earliest one easy to miss on a first pass.
- [ ] A decoy/red herring thread exists elsewhere in the sequence to absorb reader suspicion.
- [ ] After the twist event, mark which *earlier* outline events change meaning. If none do, the twist is a surprise, not a twist — reconsider it.

---

## 9. "Shown, Not Told" Readiness

Before handing an event off to be drafted as a scene:

- [ ] The event is written as an **action or exchange**, not a summary of a feeling. ("He realizes he can't trust her" → rewrite as *what happens* that forces the realization: she lies about something checkable, and he checks it.)
- [ ] The event has a **concrete setting and sensory anchor** implied, even in outline form — not floating in exposition space.
- [ ] Emotional shifts are attached to an **observable trigger** (a line said, an object found, a door closing) that a scene can dramatize, rather than a stated internal state.
- [ ] If the outline line contains an adjective describing a feeling ("she's devastated," "he's furious"), ask: *what would a camera see?* Replace or supplement the line with that.

---

## 10. Final Full-Sequence Read

- [ ] Read only the outline, no prose. Does it hold tension on its own, beat to beat?
- [ ] Every event on the list would be missed if removed. (If cutting an event changes nothing downstream, cut it now — not after it's a full scene.)
- [ ] The last event delivers on the central question from Section 1 — and costs something to get there.
