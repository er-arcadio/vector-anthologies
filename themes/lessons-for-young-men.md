# Lessons for Young Men

## Middle School Boys

### 1. Have fun — on your own terms
Don't just go along with whatever your friends think is fun. Figure out what actually makes *you* happy, and stick with it, even if it's not what everyone else is doing. Be someone who helps make good moments happen, not someone who just waits around to be entertained.

### 2. Be playful, not just logical
Don't just talk about facts and serious stuff all the time — it gets boring. Joke around. Say silly, made-up things. Guess something fun about a person ("I bet you're the kind of person who sneaks candy into the movies"). Keep it light and positive — never say something to make someone feel judged or embarrassed. The goal is a fun conversation, not a "gotcha."

### 3. Say what you want — and respect the answer
It's okay to tell someone you like them. If they say no, that's okay too — just walk away calmly instead of arguing or getting upset. This matters for two reasons: it shows you respect their answer, and it shows you respect yourself. You were honest, and you're not going to fall apart over something you can't control.

### 4. Build a posse
Have a real group of friends — guys and girls — instead of hanging all your time on one best friend or one person you like. The bigger and more real your group of friends is, the better your life feels, no matter how any one person feels about you.

### 5. Be kind for free, not for a reward
Treat people well because that's just who you are — not because you're hoping it'll make someone like you back. Here's a good test: would you do this for a guy friend? If not, ask yourself why you're doing it just for a girl you like. A lot of guys quietly think, "the more I do for her, the more she'll like me." That's not real kindness — that's hoping for something in return. And when it doesn't work out, it usually just makes you feel angry or let down. Real kindness doesn't expect anything back.

## High School

*(These build on the 5 rules above — those still apply.)*

### 1. Change the mood, not the mind
Don't take most things too seriously — especially in high school, where it's genuinely hard to tell what's a real argument and what's just banter. If someone makes a comment about you, your identity, or something you did, your first move shouldn't be to get defensive. You know who you are. Laugh at yourself, make a joke, keep it light.

Treat most moments like they're playful until proven otherwise. If it really is serious, the other person will usually make that clear — they'll stop joking around, or tell you straight up. That's your cue to switch out of joke mode and actually talk it through. But don't assume every comment is an attack you need to defend yourself from. Most of the time, it's not.

That said, don't hide behind jokes forever. If someone genuinely wants a real answer from you — not just messing around, but actually asking you to explain yourself — give them one. Keep it short and honest, then you can go back to having fun.

One more thing worth knowing: girls will tease you sometimes, and that's almost always playful — actually, it can be a sign she's into you. Don't read too much into it, just enjoy it and tease back.

Guys are different. If a guy gives you a hard time specifically in front of other people, it's usually not really about you — it's him feeling a little intimidated and testing whether you'll crack under pressure (get defensive, walk away, or give him a bunch of attention). Don't give him that. Keep your energy cool, play along lightly the first time, and if he keeps at it, just stop engaging — don't give him much eye contact or attention either. If it ever crosses into 1-on-1 bullying or gets physical, that's a different situation and should be handled as real bullying, not banter.

Either way, fighting and losing your cool are not a good look. Staying calm while the other guy loses his temper is always the stronger move — it shows you're in control of yourself, which matters a lot more than winning an argument.

### 2. Show, don't tell
Show your boundaries — don't *always* tell them. Let me explain: figure out what your boundaries actually are first. What's okay with you, what's not. Once you know them, you don't have to explain them to everyone who crosses one.

With people close to you — your best friend, a girlfriend, someone you actually trust — it's worth talking it through. But with people you don't know well, you don't owe them a breakdown of what you believe or why. Some people will poke at your boundaries just to see if they can talk you out of them. You don't need to hand them the tools to do that. Just act with integrity, hold the line, and move on.

### 3. If you're in your head, you're dead
Overthinking will wreck a good moment. Let's say you said something awkward to a girl you like, or a group of friends went quiet after you made a joke — and now you can't stop replaying it, wondering what everyone's thinking. That's being "in your head," and it'll make you spiral way more than whatever actually happened.

Even in bigger moments, the same idea applies. Maybe you did something you know was really wrong — like cheating on someone — or you're about to walk into something high-pressure and nerve-wracking, like a big party. Take a moment first: if you did something wrong, actually own it and apologize. If something big is coming up, take a second to mentally prepare. Then let it go and come back to the present. Don't carry it around with you all day.

When you catch yourself overthinking, get present. Look around. Notice what's actually happening right now — not the version you're imagining. 99 times out of 100, everyone else has already moved on, and you're the only one still stuck on it.

## College

*(These build on everything above — the 5 middle school rules and 3 high school rules still apply.)*

### 1. Loads of Guy and Girl Friends
Have guy friends *and* girl friends — they serve different purposes, and you want both. Guy friends tend to be your goofy, wild, "let's do something dumb and fun" energy — they'll pull you into adventures. Girl friends tend to keep you in check — they'll tell you straight up if you're being weird, coming on too strong, or need to relax. They also usually know where the actually good stuff is happening, since they tend to get more invites than guys do. A solid friend group has both, for both reasons.

Here's the other thing: don't expect to click with everyone you meet, and don't settle for a small circle because of it. Realistically, maybe 1 or 2 out of every 10 people you meet turn into real, ride-or-die friends — people who actually show up for you and want you around just as much as you want them around. So the move isn't to try to land 10 friends. It's to meet 100 people, so you naturally end up with 20 real ones.

It's also tempting to latch onto the first few people who are nice to you — the first five people who smile back or treat you well can feel like your whole world early on. Don't cling to them. Give that same positive energy right back, grab their contact info, keep hanging out with them — but keep meeting new people too. Let your circle expand instead of locking in early with whoever happened to be friendly first.

### 2. Go Big or Go Home
College is a short window — really just four years — where you have more freedom and more access to people close to your age than you'll likely ever have again. Work hard, but play just as hard. Don't spend it playing it safe out of fear.

Part of that is being open to exploring what you actually want — including romantically and sexually — instead of holding back or assuming no one else is interested. Chances are, plenty of people around you are looking for the same kind of experiences, or at least open to them if you ask.

The mindset that makes this work is **abundance, not scarcity**. You're surrounded by thousands of people your age — that means one "no," one awkward moment, or one thing not working out isn't the end of anything. It just means you meet someone else. Operate like there's always another person, another opportunity, another day — because in college, there genuinely is. That confidence (not desperation) is what makes people want to be around you in the first place.
