Years later, when people ask me what it felt like the first time, they mean the flying. The lifting. The party trick version. I let them mean that. It's easier than telling them the first time that mattered, I wasn't lifting anything. I was standing very still in the middle of a street that used to have a Duane Reade on the corner, and there was a sound in my ears like a tuning fork that wouldn't stop, and my hands were doing something my hands have never done before or since, which was nothing at all. Just hanging there at my sides. Empty. Steady.

The city around me had gone the particular quiet of a held breath.

That's the picture. Before I tell you what put it there, I want to tell you about the cake.

*

I turned sixteen on a Tuesday in March, and my mother made a cake with actual buttercream because the store kind, she said, tasted like a candle. We ate at the kitchen table with the good plates, the ones with the gold rim that only came out for birthdays and the one time the parish priest visited. My father sat at the head of the table the way he always did, like the chair had been assigned to him by a committee, and he watched me blow out sixteen candles and said, "Sixteen. You know what I was doing at sixteen? Working. Every day after school, my father's shop, four to close." He wasn't looking at me when he said it. He was looking at the candles, at the smoke still coming off them, like the smoke was the actual subject and I was incidental to it.

My mother put her hand flat on the table. Not on his arm. On the table, between them, like she was holding a door open that wanted badly to close.

"He's got good grades," she said. "He's got the physics thing. Let him have the cake."

"I'm letting him have the cake," my father said. "I'm having some too." And he did. He had two pieces, actually, and by the second one he was telling a story about his own father's shop that had nothing to do with me at all, and I understood, the way you understand weather, that the correction had already happened and didn't need saying twice.

On the little TV on the counter, the one my mother kept on low during dinner because she said a silent house made her nervous, the anchor was talking about a tremor. Nothing serious. Felt across three blocks in Jersey City around six-forty, no damage reported, seismologists calling it minor and unremarkable, probably settling in old infrastructure. My father made a joke about the PATH train finally doing some good for once. Nobody looked at the television. Nobody looked at me. I remember my hands feeling strange under the table, warm in a way that had nothing to do with the candles, and I remember thinking it was the cake, too much sugar too fast, and reaching for water.

I didn't know yet what six-forty was. I know now. I think about it more than I think about almost anything else from that year — not the moment itself, which I don't remember, which nobody remembers, not even me — but the fact that it happened one room away from a cake with real buttercream, and got called minor, and got forgotten by everyone in the room except, eventually, me.

My mother caught my eye across the table while my father talked. She didn't say anything. She never said anything, that whole year, that would have made things worse. She just held it, whatever it was between us, steady, the way you hold a glass that's too full to move quickly.

Later — much later, another year, a bedroom, a version of me that had stopped being surprised by what my own hands could do — she would be the only one who knew both of what I was. She never asked me to choose between them. I didn't understand yet, at sixteen, over cake, what that kind of not-asking costs a person. I understand it now the way I understand everything from that year: too late to do anything with except carry it correctly.

*

The night I kill Shockwave, six years later, I am not thinking about cake.

I am thinking about angles, containment radius, the half-life of his discharges, whether the block has been cleared far enough back. I am thinking the way I have trained myself to think, which is to say: I am not thinking about the person, because the person is currently seven feet tall and the sick, saturated yellow of something gone wrong at the cellular level, and losing pieces of the sidewalk every time he flinches from pain that does not stop, has not stopped in nine minutes, will apparently not stop until either he does or I do.

His name is not Shockwave. I know his name. I looked it up two years ago, back when he was still a guy with a job and a shaking hand and a doctor who told him it was probably stress. I have carried his name around since then the way you carry a fact you hope never to need.

Two buildings down, a woman is still in a third-floor apartment because the fire escape buckled under its own panic and the ladder truck can't get an angle through the parked cars. I can feel her — not see her, feel her, the electromagnetic noise of a person too scared to make a sound, which has its own signature if you know how to read it, and by now I know how to read almost everything. I put a shield across her window a half-second before the next discharge goes wide and blows the corner off a bus shelter into particulate. Glass and aluminum, atomized mid-flight. I am, in this specific and narrow way, very good at my job.

He is not. He never wanted this job. Nobody trained him for it. He tried, before any of this, to get someone to listen to what was happening inside him, and the someone was a doctor with elevated liability insurance and eleven minutes per appointment, and there was no hotline, because why would there be, this world didn't have a name yet for what he was turning into. He is not evil. I want to be extremely clear with myself about this, then and now: he is a man who asked for help on a Tuesday and got a pamphlet about stress management, and eleven months later he is standing in the middle of Jersey City taking the block apart one flinch at a time because his body has stopped listening to him the way mine, for now, still listens to me.

I could reach him with a shield. I could reach him with cold, freeze the reaction at the joint before it discharges, buy thirty seconds, forty. I have bought thirty seconds four times already tonight and each time the reaction comes back angrier, because the thing underneath him is not slowing down, it is accelerating, and every minute he stays conscious inside it is a minute closer to something none of the four of us on this street will walk away from.

There is a version of this where I get close enough, and I am close enough now, and I say his name.

I say it first. Before anything else. I say it the way you'd say it to him at a diner, ordinary, like I'm asking him to pass the sugar, because I want the last thing that happens to him to happen to a person and not to a phenomenon.

He looks at me when I say it. Something moves behind the wrongness of his face that I recognize, that I will spend a long time afterward trying not to recognize, because recognizing it means admitting I saw him clearly in the exact second before I stopped him being anything at all.

The deconstruction doesn't feel like effort. That's the part nobody tells you, that the worst thing you will ever do with your hands might not cost you anything to do. It costs me everything after. In the moment itself it is the easiest thing I have done all night — easier than the shield, easier than the cold — a loosening, a letting-go of the bonds holding a very specific, very tired arrangement of matter into the shape of a man, and then he isn't an arrangement anymore. He is fog. Warm, briefly luminous, drifting sideways on a wind that wasn't there a second ago and won't be there a second from now.

The street goes quiet the way a held breath is quiet.

I stand in it. My hands are empty at my sides. Not shaking. That's the thing I remember, the thing that scares me more in the retelling than it did at the time: my hands do not shake. They just hang there, steady, having done the thing they did, waiting to be asked to do something else.

Somewhere behind me, sirens are still arriving for a fire that's already out.

It comes to me standing there — not as an idea, more as a temperature change, a wrongness arriving late to a room it should have been in from the start — that I had the other thing available to me. The reorganizing, not the dissolving. I had matter creation at a stage where I could have reached for it, slow and deliberate and probably not fast enough, but *there*, a real option sitting unused in a drawer I didn't open because I never thought to look in it under fire. I could have tried to put him back together as something that wasn't in pain. I don't know if it would have worked. I will never know if it would have worked. What I know is that the knowledge of the drawer arrived about four seconds after I needed it, which in this line of work is the same as it never having existed at all.

I don't say any of this to the two officers who ask me, gently, careful with a twenty-two-year-old who just did what I did, if I'm all right.

I say yes.

*

That night, in my apartment, before the story breaks, before my father says what he says about it at dinner two days later without knowing whose hands he's talking about, I sit on the edge of my bed for a long time without turning on a light.

My old physics teacher has a phrase he likes, one he's said to me so many times it's stopped sounding like a sentence and started sounding like weather: *nothing is perfect.* He means it about equations, mostly. Rounding errors, friction nobody accounted for, the gap between the model and the thing the model is supposed to describe. I don't think he means it about this. But it's the only thing in my head that fits in the space where an explanation should be, so I sit with it instead, the way you sit with a phrase in a language you don't quite speak, hoping the meaning arrives if you wait long enough.

It doesn't, that night. It still hasn't, some nights.

I don't cry. I want to say I did, because it would make a better story, a cleaner one — the boy who kills for the first time and finally breaks, finally feels it the way you're supposed to feel it. What actually happens is smaller and worse: I sit on the edge of the bed with my hands open on my knees, watching them do nothing, and somewhere very far off, a tremor that nobody will connect to anything for years is exactly what it has always been, which is a thing that already happened, quietly, while everyone in the room was looking somewhere else.
